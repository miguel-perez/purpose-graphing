# Summarize the speaker's thinking or feeling

Express what the quote supports from inside the speaker's experience. Preserve the meaning behind what they do or refrain from doing, not merely the events. Do not supply a plausible motive, emotion, or outcome that the speaker does not express.

## Recognize what can be summarized

Distinguish five kinds of content:

- **Mind at a particular past moment:** what the speaker notices, thinks, weighs, intends, decides, or feels during an actual experience. A personal rule belongs here when it guides their choice then.
- **Mind-like content without a particular past moment:** thinking spread over several occasions, imagined responses, predictions about situations not experienced, or a state the reader infers rather than the speaker expresses.
- **Relationship to a topic:** approval, disapproval, preferences, settled opinions, and habits.
- **Circumstances:** surroundings, actions, events, practical explanations, and plain facts.
- **Other content:** what does not fit these distinctions.

Summarize experienced thinking or feeling, or an expressed personal rule for choosing. Other content can explain what that thinking concerns or why it matters, but cannot by itself establish a motive or emotion.

### Do not require a formal statement of thought

The speaker need not say “I thought” or supply a date. Present-tense language can explain a response during an experience. A general-sounding statement can express the purpose of an activity or a rule the speaker uses.

Read the meaning of the whole quote:
- Does the speaker evaluate something they encounter?
- Do they explain what they are trying to achieve or avoid?
- Do they describe a choice together with its reason?
- Do they express a condition that guides their choices?
- Do their own words convey urgency, anticipation, reluctance, or another state without naming it formally?

An action with an expressed reason can support a summary of the choice. An action alone cannot establish an unspoken reason. Someone else's demand alone does not establish how the speaker feels about it.

Quote: “The clerk needed an answer before closing. To me it was all too sudden; I still hadn't worked out what I wanted.”

Possible summary: “Feel rushed to choose before closing when I have not worked out what I want.”

The speaker's own assessment supports feeling rushed. Without that assessment, do not invent distress merely because there is a deadline.

### Distinguish missing context from missing meaning

A short or unfinished quote may still contain a complete thought. Summarize the supported part without completing the speaker's unfinished sentence.

Uncertainty about a minor fact does not erase a clear purpose or decision. Preserve that uncertainty if the fact matters; otherwise omit the uncertain detail. Do not turn a possible means into an established one.

Conversely, a quote consisting only of movement, attendance, or another plain action does not establish enjoyment, motivation, or a decision rule. If no eligible meaning remains after checking, do not manufacture one.

## Steps

1. **Locate the thinking or feeling.**  
   Identify what the speaker wants, notices, believes, weighs, chooses, or feels. Separate their own state from facts and from other people's thoughts. Use only the supplied quote, including its fragments.

2. **Choose the center.**  
   Find the thought or feeling that best explains the quote as a whole. Do not automatically lead with the first statement. When a discovery enables a choice and the speaker explains that choice, usually lead with the choice. When concern develops into tentative trust, the later stance may be the center, with the concern retained only as needed.

3. **Keep the defining connection.**  
   Preserve the object and the important reason, purpose, condition, or contrast. Include a concrete means when it explains how the speaker pursues the purpose. Do not replace distinctive meaning with a broad word such as enjoyment, connection, or uncertainty.

4. **Match the speaker's stance.**  
   Keep consequential qualifications: trying rather than committing, preferring rather than requiring, suspecting rather than knowing. Preserve negation, uncertainty, intensity, and personal scope. A decision about what to do is not the same as a judgment about what is true.

5. **Write from inside the experience.**  
   Begin with a present-tense verb that names the thinking or feeling, with the initial “I” understood. Put what it concerns next, then the defining reason or condition. Use “I,” “my,” “me,” or “we” within the sentence as appropriate. Prefer a direct verb over a wrapper such as “Think that I want” or “Feel that I believe.”

6. **Compress without flattening.**  
   Default to one coherent sentence. Keep linked reasoning, not a sequence of everything mentioned. Remove repetition and incidental background. Keep another thought only when it is necessary to express a contrast, a change, or an inseparable part of the same meaning.

## Fragile distinctions

### Lead with the choice when the quote explains a choice

Do not summarize only the information that made a decision possible.

Quote: “We'd forgotten the spade. Then we noticed the tool library was open, so we borrowed one there.”

Possible summary: “Decide to borrow a spade from the open tool library because we have forgotten ours.”

The missing tool explains borrowing, not noticing the opening hours. Attach each reason to the thought or choice it actually explains.

Do not turn every completed action into “Decide.” Use that verb when choosing is supported by the speaker's explanation. Likewise, do not weaken an expressed effort to ensure something into a mere wish.

Quote: “Before the frost arrived, I checked every cover because I wanted none of the young plants exposed.”

Possible summary: “Make sure every young plant is covered before the frost.”

### Keep agency clear

Distinguish proposing, choosing, agreeing, and following someone else's choice. Do not assign the speaker a decision made by another person. If the quote supports shared intention followed by another person's selection, preserve that relationship without adding enthusiasm, reluctance, or deference.

Going along with a choice can be the relevant meaning when the speaker connects it to their own reasons. Mere attendance does not establish approval.

### Choose a precise verb without strengthening the meaning

Use a verb for the state actually expressed:
- An intention may warrant “Want” or “Plan.”
- Weighing alternatives may warrant “Consider.”
- A judgment may warrant “Think,” “Recognize,” or “Expect.”
- A choice may warrant “Choose,” “Decide,” or “Decline.”
- A supported purpose may warrant “Try to” or “Make sure.”

These are not interchangeable. A preference does not become a firm commitment. A conclusion about personal limits does not necessarily mean a permanent refusal.

For an emotion, normally begin with “Feel” immediately followed by the emotion. An emotional verb such as “Enjoy,” “Worry,” “Hope,” or “Regret” can be more direct. “Feel that…” often introduces a judgment rather than an emotion.

Quote: “The missing sketchbook turned up under the seat. I was so relieved.”

Possible summary: “Feel relieved to find my missing sketchbook under the seat.”

Do not strengthen relief into joy or a judgment about risk into fear.

### Express the current stance, not a miniature history

When thinking changes, lead with the stance that organizes the quote's conclusion. Retain earlier uncertainty if it still qualifies that stance.

Quote: “At first I doubted the patch would hold. The mechanic explained the test, and I was willing to rely on it, though not completely.”

Possible summary: “Trust the patch cautiously after the mechanic explains the test.”

This retains the qualified trust without recounting every stage. Do not force a change when the quote leaves two positions unresolved.

Keep the speaker inside the relevant moment throughout the sentence. Earlier circumstances can remain earlier; not every verb must be present tense.

### Keep reasons, contrasts, and scope faithful

Use “because,” “despite,” or “so” only where the relationship is supported. Nearby statements are not automatically causes of one another.

- Do not invent an alternative the speaker rejects.
- Do not rank several reasons unless the speaker does.
- Do not convert a main reason into an absolute requirement.
- Do not turn another person's possible response into the speaker's own emotion.
- Do not assert an outcome merely because the speaker expects or hopes for it.

A reason can be clear from the speaker's explanation without a formal causal word. Preserve that reasoning, but not a stronger claim.

### Keep enough concrete meaning

Replace unclear references only when the quote supplies what they refer to. Keep a distinctive activity, obstacle, or means instead of retaining only a broad benefit.

Quote: “I joined the repair sessions to learn how to fix my own kettle rather than keep replacing it.”

Possible summary: “Want to learn kettle repair so I can fix mine instead of replacing it.”

Names and locations matter when they identify the object of the thinking; they need not survive when they are incidental. Never supply an absent identity, place, or purpose from outside the quote.

### Keep rules personal and preferences proportionate

A personal rule explains how the speaker guides their choices, not merely how a process works. Preserve its condition and purpose without inventing its origin or a particular occasion of use.

Quote: “If a task is unfamiliar, I ask someone to watch my first attempt so I don't repeat a mistake.”

Possible summary: “Ask someone to watch my first attempt at an unfamiliar task to avoid repeating a mistake.”

Do not reject an expressed rule simply because it applies repeatedly. Equally, do not inflate an isolated choice into a lifelong principle.

A preference can explain an attraction or purpose in the experience. Summarize that expressed meaning without inventing a decision. General-sounding language does not require either rejecting the quote or making it more definite.

## Check the draft

- Does the opening verb name the central thought, feeling, or choice rather than incidental background?
- Is this the speaker's perspective, with their agency and degree of certainty intact?
- Does the summary retain the distinctive object and defining connection?
- Does every reason attach to the right thought or choice?
- Have any emotions, motives, identities, requirements, or outcomes been added?
- Can repetition or a trailing event be removed without losing meaning?
- If the quote seems unsummarizable, have I checked for an expressed assessment, purpose, choice, or personal rule without filling in missing context?
