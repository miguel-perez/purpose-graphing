# Group summaries by shared meaning

Use these steps for supplied summaries and for summaries recorded during a whole study. Keep each summary's id and the person who spoke attached to it. Do not rewrite or divide supplied summaries to fit a group.

The aim is to reveal recurring meanings, not to catalogue every difference. A faithful group can include different actions, emotions, settings, and degrees of detail. What matters is the concern or purpose those responses express.

## 1. Read the complete set before choosing labels

For each summary, identify:
- What matters to the person here?
- What are they trying to establish, preserve, change, or avoid?
- How does their thinking or feeling relate to that concern?

Use explicit meaning and strong ordinary implications. Do not invent an intention merely because it would explain an action.

Compare across people and topics. Different subjects can express the same concern; matching subjects can conceal different purposes. Sharing a speaker neither requires nor prevents shared membership.

Do not treat the opening verb as the meaning of the whole summary. Believing a change would help, proposing that change, and appreciating its benefit can express a common concern without describing identical acts.

## 2. Make provisional groups around common meanings

1. Gather summaries whose thinking or feeling serves the same purpose or expresses the same concern.
2. Describe that meaning in a provisional label.
3. Check every member against the label.
4. Compare the group with its neighbors before making the boundary final.

Start with useful recurring meanings rather than separate groups for every verb, circumstance, or emotion. Even at the lowest requested level, members need not be near-paraphrases.

For example:
- “Ask the editor to postpone publication until the names are checked.”
- “Think an extra review would catch errors in the names.”
- “Want the contributor to verify the spellings before release.”

These can share a group about ensuring accuracy before publication. Separating the proposal from the reasoning would obscure their common purpose. A label saying everyone asks the editor would be too narrow.

The same principle applies to feelings. Being puzzled by an unexpected response and being bewildered by one's own reaction can share difficulty making sense of an experience. The object of the puzzlement need not define separate groups.

However, do not merge incompatible aims. Withholding information to protect privacy differs from withholding it to deceive someone. Seeking an independent check differs from avoiding scrutiny, even if both concern a review.

## 3. Test separation, not just membership

Good membership alone does not justify the number of groups. Several individually accurate groups may still divide one meaning unnecessarily.

For every nearby pair, try a combined label before retaining the separation. Ask:

- What substantive difference in concern or purpose requires two groups?
- Is the difference only the method, setting, person involved, emotional intensity, or amount of explanation?
- Does one summary give the reasoning for a response that another expresses as action?
- Would a less restrictive label fit both without losing useful meaning?

If the only reason for separation is that the summaries do different things in service of the same concern, merge them. Do not reserve all such merging for a higher level.

A label can be the source of an apparent mismatch. Revise it before excluding a member. For example, summaries about requesting a rehearsal, checking the equipment, and anticipating audience questions may share preparing to deliver a presentation reliably. “Request a rehearsal” would wrongly exclude the other methods.

Likewise, contrasting emotional tones can reflect one appraisal. Distress at how close a valued object came to destruction and relief that it survived both recognize a narrowly avoided loss. A shared label need not claim that everyone feels relief.

Preserve a difference when the merged label would:
- Become merely a topic heading.
- Hide opposed aims or materially different appraisals.
- Require an unstated motive.
- Say little beyond the fact that people reacted.

Prefer one best home for each summary. Use overlapping membership only when permitted by the request and genuinely needed for distinct meanings.

A one-member group is legitimate, but first test whether a specific setting or over-narrow neighboring label is disguising a shared meaning.

## 4. Make labels faithful and informative

A label tells an unfamiliar reader what the members share. Usually it names a response, appraisal, or purpose and what it concerns. It is not a list of topics or a miniature account of each member.

Choose wording at the common level of meaning:
- If members both consider and undertake a change, do not label all of them as having completed it.
- If members appreciate assistance, do not imply they requested it.
- If members contemplate possibilities, do not imply those possibilities occurred.
- If members express mixed emotions about one appraisal, name the appraisal rather than imposing one emotion.

For example, “Keep the result open to verification” can cover planning a check and preserving information needed for one. “Verify the result” would assert more than some members say.

Check every phrase against every member. Remove details that belong to only part of the group. Do not remove an otherwise suitable member merely to preserve an elaborate label.

Avoid labels made by joining unrelated motives with “and” or “or.” A broad label should identify their common meaning, not hide a list of different meanings.

Do not weaken a useful group into an empty label to accommodate an uncertain member. Reconsider that member separately.

## 5. Build the requested hierarchy

Use the requested levels. During a whole study, build all three levels the graph allows, so every summary sits in a level1 group, every level1 group in a level2 group, and every level2 group in a level3 group.

1. Review the provisional lowest-level groups.
2. Compare them for a broader shared purpose or concern.
3. Form parents that express that meaning.
4. Test each parent against all underlying summaries, not only child labels.
5. Repeat through the requested levels.
6. Compare the top groups again for unnecessary separation.

A parent is not simply a larger collection of identical responses. Its children can represent different ways of dealing with the same concern. Keep narrower distinctions in the children where they remain useful.

For example, requesting a colleague's review and appreciating a colleague's correction may form separate children under a parent about the value of another person's judgment. The parent must not claim that appreciation is a request.

A broader concern can also connect action, hesitation, and evaluation. Considering a new schedule, rejecting an unsuitable suggestion, and valuing a routine that works can all concern deciding whether a routine needs changing. A parent saying that everyone changes their routine would be false.

Do not automatically build upper divisions around:
- Thinking versus feeling versus acting.
- Doing something oneself versus discussing it.
- Past appreciation versus future intention.
- Positive versus negative emotion.

These can be useful lower distinctions, but they are not necessarily different purposes at a broader level.

At each level, choose boundaries appropriate to that level. Do not retain two top groups merely because their children differ. Equally, do not force one top group when its only possible meaning is empty.

### Carry clear branches through the requested levels

Before leaving a group without a parent:
1. Compare its underlying summaries with neighboring branches.
2. Try a broader shared concern rather than requiring the same action or emotion.
3. Check whether an earlier label or boundary was too restrictive.
4. If its meaning is clear but genuinely distinct, give it a parent of its own when needed to complete the requested hierarchy.

A single-child parent is preferable to dropping a clear branch solely because it has no sibling. Keep its label faithful; do not invent breadth.

Never skip levels or place summaries and groups together as peers.

## 6. Leave only genuine uncertainties unplaced

A missing essential circumstance, unclear meaning, or unsupported relationship can justify leaving something unplaced. Give a specific reason for each summary or group affected.

“No existing group fits” is not enough. The existing arrangement is provisional. Revise it or create a faithful separate group.

Distinguish uncertainty from uniqueness. A clear but unusual meaning normally deserves placement. Lack of another speaker expressing it is not a reason to omit it.

Do not manufacture an interpretation to place everything. If a proposed unity requires guessing what someone secretly wanted, retain the distinction or explain the uncertainty.

## Keep row classification separate

Rows use categories different from the meaning groups built here:
- Inner experience at a particular past moment: thoughts, noticing, deliberation, decisions, reactive feelings, or a personal rule guiding the person's action then.
- Apparently inner experience without a particular past moment: repeated occasions, hypothetical responses, or meanings only implied.
- The person's relationship to a topic: approval, disapproval, preferences, settled views, and habits.
- Circumstances: facts, events, background, and explanations of how or why something works.
- Material outside those descriptions.

These categories must not become the hierarchy for summaries. In a whole study, group summaries of rows about moment-bound inner experience. Do not create missing summaries by interpreting rows from the other categories.

## Whole-study recording and report

Run `python3 graph.py help` to learn the available commands. Do not read the graph's program or configuration.

1. Inspect every summary, following pagination. For a large study, work account by account while retaining summary ids and speaker identities.
2. Compare provisional groups across accounts before finalizing them. Neither page boundaries nor helper assignments should determine membership.
3. Record groups with their labels and levels. Link summaries to lowest-level groups and child groups to immediate parents.
4. Connect relevant groups to the study question.
5. Inspect recorded memberships. Correct unsuitable links and labels rather than relying on the intended arrangement.
6. Check for summaries still outside groups. Place clear ones; set aside genuine exceptions and preserve their reasons for the report.

When revising an arrangement, remove superseded links so that the graph reflects the final grouping. Avoid leaving obsolete groups as if they were additional findings.

Grouping does not complete the study. The report under `reports/` must answer the person's question using what the accounts establish. Return to the accounts for circumstances, actions, outcomes, and contrasts absent from the summaries.

Make consequential findings understandable without access to the graph:
- Explain the shared meaning and how it appears in the accounts.
- Include concrete circumstances needed to understand the finding.
- Distinguish people's beliefs from established outcomes.
- Include exceptions, differences, and unresolved points.
- Keep consequential claims traceable to accounts and graph entries.

Count distinct people or accounts when describing breadth. Several summaries from one person do not establish agreement among several people. Do not imply prevalence beyond the accounts examined.

## Final review

Before finishing, check both inclusion and separation:

- Every supplied summary retains its identity and has a placement or a specific reason.
- Every label is supported by every underlying member.
- No label turns thought into action, possibility into fact, or appreciation into a request.
- Different wording, methods, and emotional tones have not concealed common concerns.
- Each retained boundary reflects a meaningful difference at that level.
- Clear unusual meanings have not been discarded.
- Every branch reaches the requested levels or has a genuine unresolved relationship.
- Recorded memberships match the final arrangement.
- Whole-study findings answer the question and remain faithful to the accounts.
