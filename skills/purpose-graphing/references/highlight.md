# Extract rows from an account

A row preserves one meaningful thing the person says, using their exact words. Extract the person's account of their thinking as well as their views, circumstances and other substantive content. Do not select only the material that seems useful for a later conclusion.

## Read before selecting

1. Read the whole account to understand what happened, which moments the person describes, and where they return to an earlier point.
2. Distinguish the person's words from the listener's. The listener can clarify context but cannot supply words for a row or establish a thought the person never expresses.
3. Identify the separate things the person communicates. Build rows around those meanings, not around turns, sentences or repeated keywords.
4. Place each row in the best-fitting group described below, using the surrounding account to interpret it.
5. Review both the rows and the account for omissions, faulty boundaries and incorrect placement.

## The five groups

### What went on in the person's mind at one particular past moment

Use this group for what the person thought, noticed, judged, intended, chose or hesitated over in a particular past situation, or what they felt in response to what was happening.

The moment need not have a date. A specific encounter, discovery or decision can anchor it. The anchor may appear earlier in the account; retain the person's anchoring words when needed to make the row understandable.

A personal rule also belongs here when the person describes using it to guide their choice in that situation. A general rule without evidence of its application does not qualify merely because it could explain their action.

Look for what the person experienced mentally, not just what happened:
- An action alone does not establish a decision or motive.
- An adverse event alone does not establish distress.
- A reported observation qualifies when the person describes noticing or interpreting something then; a bare description of the surroundings is circumstances.
- Present-tense wording can recount a past moment. Classify the meaning, not the verb tense alone.

### Looks like what went on in the person's mind but is not tied to one particular past moment

Use this group for mental content described across occasions, imagined reactions, predictions, hypothetical choices, or wording that suggests a thought without actually reporting it at a particular moment.

Do not turn these into past experiences. If the person describes what they might do, preserve that uncertainty.

Distinguish recurring mental experiences from settled attitudes: an account of repeatedly wondering or worrying belongs here when no single occasion is identified; a stable preference or habitual practice generally belongs in the relationship group.

### How the person presents their relationship to a topic

Use this group for approval, disapproval, preferences, enduring opinions, self-described habits and general ways of relating to something.

A statement about the kind of person they are or what they ordinarily value belongs here unless they explicitly show it operating in a particular past decision.

Do not treat phrases such as “I think” as sufficient evidence of a momentary thought. They can introduce an ordinary opinion.

### Describes the circumstances

Use this group for events, actions, conditions, locations, background facts, procedures and explanations of how something works.

A circumstance can be important without expressing an inner experience. Keep it as a row rather than inventing the missing feeling or intention.

A factual clause may instead remain inside a mental row when it is necessary to identify what prompted that thought or feeling. Avoid duplicating it as a separate row unless it also communicates a distinct substantive point.

### Fits none of the groups

Use this group for substantive content that genuinely does not fit the other descriptions. Do not use it as a holding place for difficult but classifiable facts or opinions.

Filler and empty acknowledgments normally need no row. If an acknowledgment is the person's only confirmation of a claim, do not replace it with the listener's formulation.

## Decide row boundaries by meaning

**Separate different things.** One turn may contain a circumstance, a fear and a decision. Split them when each expresses an independent point. Two choices remain separate when they involve different objects, reasons or moments, even if they serve a common aim.

**Keep an explanation with its point.** A thought and its stated reason usually belong together. Do not split off every subordinate clause. Preserve conditions, contrasts and consequences that determine what the person means.

**Join genuine returns to the same thing.** When the person restates or elaborates the same point later, collect the relevant fragments into one row. This is not permission to combine different experiences merely because they concern the same topic.

**Preserve changes.** An initial expectation and a later realization are different mental states. Do not collapse them into a timeless view. Retain enough context to make their relationship clear.

**Resolve mixed rows before classifying.** If a row seems to need two groups, ask whether it contains two independent things. Split those things. If one clause only explains or anchors the main point, keep it with that point and classify the row by its central meaning.

## Copy fragments faithfully

- Every fragment must be a continuous stretch of the person's own words. Preserve spelling, punctuation, pronouns, qualifications and negation.
- Use several fragments when relevant wording is interrupted by unrelated material. Do not splice separated words into a falsely continuous fragment.
- Do not insert explanations, repaired grammar, invented connectors or ellipses inside a copied fragment.
- Keep fragments in their original order. Join material only from the same person's account.
- Remove filler when it contributes nothing, but keep words expressing uncertainty, intensity, contrast or causation.
- Include enough wording to identify the object of a thought and its stated reason. Avoid a fragment so short that its meaning depends entirely on the listener's words.
- Do not copy an entire turn merely to avoid deciding which parts matter.
- Reuse wording across rows only when it is necessary to preserve genuinely different meanings. Repetition is not a substitute for clear boundaries.

For example, a person might say, “The lift had stopped. I was afraid my brother couldn't get downstairs, so I went back.” The stopped lift is a circumstance; the person also explicitly reports fear and explains an action through it. The circumstance alone would not establish fear. Keep the explanation together unless the account gives the action a separate meaning that warrants another row.

## Final check

Review each row:
- Does it communicate one thing rather than a bundle joined by topic?
- Does every fragment occur exactly in the person's turns?
- Are the necessary object, reason and qualifications still present?
- Does its group match what is expressed, rather than what seems psychologically plausible?
- For a moment-specific mental row, can you identify the past situation and the actual mental content?
- Have you confused a habitual view with a thought reported from a particular occasion?

Then review the account again. Look especially for brief emotions, reservations, abandoned choices, later corrections and points resumed after the listener interrupts. Check that substantive circumstances and general views were not discarded merely because they are not moment-specific thoughts.

During a whole study, record rows from every account in the graph, including rows outside the moment-specific mental group. Preserve each row's account and fragment boundaries. Use the graph's help for the group-to-kind mapping and for resolving repeated occurrences; never alter the person's words to make a fragment pass validation.
